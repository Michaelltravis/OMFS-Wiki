# Jacobs proposal voice guide

Content-bank standard for proposal prose. Version 1.1, 2026-09-06 (supersedes v1.0 of 2026-09-05: hard gates recalibrated to the winners' floor, every rule test cut to one line, hedged facts in the AFTER pairs written around instead of asserted).

Derived from three measured lens analyses (rhythm and sentence; proof and persuasion; client address and voice) of two winning Jacobs O&M proposals, Hull WWTF (`verbatim/hull-wwtf-om-2026/pages/p0003–p0030`) and Santa Monica SWIP (`verbatim/santamonica-swip-om-2025/pages/p0004–p0034`), against our Richmond pilot (`Desktop/Richmond/Draft Sections/02 Claude Working Drafts/_work/draft_03_qualifications_edited.md`), with the tool metrics in `voice/samples/comparison.md` and `voice/samples/metrics_*.json`, and the brand voice rules in `end-game-sales-suite/skills/jacobs-documents/SKILL.md` ("Voice rules").

**Authority.** `voice/exemplar/` holds only its README (checked 2026-09-06); no exemplar document has been supplied. Until one is, the Hull and Santa Monica sections are the primary authority for voice and the Jacobs brand rules are the authority for typography and naming. When an exemplar lands, re-run the three lenses against it, record every difference in a "Where the exemplar differs" section at the end of this file, and let the exemplar win on conflicts.

**Where the brand rules and the winners differ, this guide resolves it as follows.**

## 1. The voice in one paragraph

The Jacobs proposal voice is a senior operator talking to a client it already respects. The client's situation opens every section, Jacobs answers in the second sentence, and every claim after that carries a name, a number, a date, or a named tool. Sentences run about 20 words and paragraphs one or two sentences; each paragraph closes on what the client gains, often as a triad with the serial comma and the outcome clause in bold. Headings are benefit labels, not RFP echoes; bold marks outcomes, not topics; exhibits repeat proof the prose has already stated and are pointed to by name. Commitments are promises with an owner and a cadence ("Project Manager Nathan Callison will maintain open day-to-day coordination"), never present-tense descriptions. The competition is "the previous contract operator"; the client's needs are aspirations, never deficits; required negatives are disclosed in four moves and then left alone. Nothing in the body narrates the document, argues with the reader, or hedges a fact. The writing is confident because every sentence is checkable, not because it is loud.

## 2. Rules

Thirty-five rules in nine groups. Each ends with a one-line **Test** a judge can apply without interpretation. Numeric thresholds are calibrated in Section 4, which marks each as a hard gate or directional. Measurements quoted as "winners" are Hull and Santa Monica; "pilot" is the Richmond qualifications draft.

### Sentences & rhythm

**1. Sentence length band.** Median 18–25 words (aim 20–24); p90 ≤ 40 (aim ≤ 38); no body sentence over 50 words unless it is legal or financial boilerplate copied from corporate. Winners: median 21.5 and 18.0, p90 38 and 37, nothing above 50 outside boilerplate. Pilot: p90 60, with a 63-word reference opener and a 56-word section opener.
**Test:** sort body sentences by word count and fail if any non-boilerplate sentence exceeds 50 words or p90 exceeds 40.

**2. Punch sentences are verb-bearing, sit at paragraph edges, and pair with a long sentence.** A punch sentence has ≤ 8 words, Jacobs or the client as subject, a finite verb, and first or last position in its paragraph: "Jacobs delivers on all fronts." "The onsite team will never operate alone." They are 5–10% of sentences. A bold run-in fragment ("California depth.") is not one. Every section-opening and section-closing paragraph carries an adjacent pair of one sentence ≤ 9 words and one ≥ 18: "The Town seeks a collaborative partner to improve accountability, compliance, asset stewardship, resiliency, and community engagement. Jacobs delivers on all fronts."
**Test:** list every ≤ 8-word sentence, strike any without a finite verb or not at a paragraph edge, then confirm a ≤ 9 / ≥ 18 adjacent pair in the first and last paragraph of each section.

**3. Paragraph size.** Body paragraphs average 30–45 words and never exceed 110; at least 55% have one or two sentences. Winners: 41.5 and 31.6 words on average, 59% and 73% one-or-two-sentence paragraphs. Pilot: 63.7 average, 21 of 30 paragraphs with three or more sentences, seven over 100 words.
**Test:** count words and sentences per paragraph and fail on any paragraph over 110 words or a section average over 55.

**4. Triads with the serial comma.** The house closing rhythm is three client outcomes joined "X, Y, and Z" ("compliance, reliability, and transparency"; "safe, reliable, and forward-looking wastewater services"): at least 8 per 1,000 words (Hull 17, Santa Monica 12, pilot 0.5) and at least one in every section-closing paragraph. The serial comma is never dropped ("Operations, Maintenance, Collections, Laboratory and Administration" fails).
**Test:** count "A, B, and C" constructions per 1,000 words (≥ 8) and fail any list of three or more items without the serial comma.

**5. Punctuation budget.** Per 1,000 words of body prose: colons ≤ 6, semicolons ≤ 4, em dashes ≤ 9 (aim ≤ 3, spaced per brand), square brackets 0. A colon is a payoff, not a list launcher ("We treat communication as an operational control: the right information will reach the right decision-maker at the right time"); a sentence never carries both a colon and a semicolon list of three or more items, which become bullets; at most one em-dash pair per paragraph. Winners: 3 / 1.6 / 1.3 (Hull) and 4.7 / 3.8 / 9.0 (Santa Monica). Pilot: 15.8 / 10.7 / 10.2, with 14.9 brackets.
**Test:** count each mark per 1,000 words against the budget and flag any sentence containing a colon plus two or more semicolons.

**6. Actor-first openers and lexical transitions; no meta-narration.** At least 30% of body paragraphs open with Jacobs / We / Our [noun] / a named person and reach a present- or future-tense verb within six words ("Jacobs will develop…", "Nathan is supported by…"); client-first openers belong to the first paragraph under a heading. Paragraphs are carried forward by repeating the prior sentence's key noun (objectives → objectives; data → dashboards → visibility) or by an exhibit pointer ("as shown in Exhibit 3-2"); "From Day 1,", "Beyond…", and "Building on…" are allowed openers. No sentence takes the section, the argument, or "what follows" as its subject.
**Test:** tally paragraph openers (≥ 30% actor-first) and fail on any hit for "this section", "what follows", "set(s) … apart", "the question … is", "organized around", "is here because", or "two facts".

### Headings & structure

**7. Heading density.** One heading or sub-label per 100–220 words of running prose, aiming at 130–180; every page carries at least one. Winners: 148.6 and 157.9 words per heading by the tool (112 and 85 when italic sub-labels are counted). Pilot: 281, with five of eleven headings bare project names. Write sub-labels as markdown headings (`####`) so the tool counts them and the template can style them.
**Test:** count words between consecutive headings and fail on any gap over 220 or a section averaging under 100.

**8. Headings are benefit labels in sentence case, and one per section names the client.** A heading is a 3–9-word noun phrase naming a capability or benefit ("Leadership-driven operations and accountability", "Proven operations in coastal and complex systems", "Legal standing in California"), never an RFP question stem. Digits appear only where the RFP mandates numbering, and then a benefit phrase follows the number ("3.1 One California corporation, backed by Jacobs"). At least one heading per major section names the client or its state ("Jacobs' value to Santa Monica", "EPA Region 1 experience"). Second-tier sub-labels are italic, 2–6 words. Pilot: "3.1 Corporate profile and legal qualifications", "3.5 Litigation and contract termination".
**Test:** scan the heading list and fail any heading lacking a benefit or capability noun, carrying RFP question wording or an unmandated digit, or any section with no client or state name in a heading.

**9. Assertion headings are rationed.** At most one claim-bearing heading per major section, ≤ 10 words, as an italic tagline directly under the label ("The Town of Hull is ready for a change.") or an em-dash compound ("Our role — an extension of City staff"; "More than an operator — integrated O&M + engineering"). Fewer than one heading in five carries a verb; the rest are labels. The pilot's one italic deck ran 30 words and three claims.
**Test:** count headings with a finite verb (≤ 20%) and claim-bearing headings per section (≤ 1, ≤ 10 words).

**10. Run-in bold belongs to bullets, not the spine; lists are parallel.** A bold lead-in is ≤ 6 words, followed by an en dash or colon and a 15–40-word sentence, and it governs one bullet only ("Proven Performance – Jacobs has delivered over 300…"). Any run-in longer than six words, or one that introduces more than one paragraph, is promoted to a sub-heading on its own line. Lists run 3–8 items in parallel grammar (all "We will…" or all noun phrases); numbered lists are for sequenced steps only; no more than two consecutive paragraphs share the same opening syntax. Pilot: three subsections built on 13 run-ins up to 14 words long, and five reference paragraphs in one mould (Name + is + comparative, then "lesson for Richmond").
**Test:** count bold run-ins over six words or governing more than one paragraph (0), lists with mixed grammar or more than eight items (0), and runs of three paragraphs with the same opening syntax (0).

**11. Section opener and closer shape.** A section opens with two or three paragraphs of 40–70 words: (1) the client's situation or asset with three or more numbers and a triad of pressures, (2) "Jacobs will / Jacobs brings…" ending on a bolded outcome triad, (3) a pointer to the exhibit that follows. First sentence ≤ 30 words, first paragraph ≤ 70, no bracket in the first two paragraphs. A section closes with a 40–70-word next-chapter paragraph ("By selecting Jacobs, the Town of Hull positions itself to strengthen leadership, improve transparency, and align capital investment with long-term performance.") naming the client, the lead person or team, and three outcomes; litigation, termination, and legal text sit before it, never last.
**Test:** check the first paragraph of every section (≤ 70 words, first sentence ≤ 30, no bracket) and its last body paragraph (client + lead person or team + three outcomes; not a table, disclaimer, or bracket).

### Proof & consequence

**12. Comparator in the same sentence.** Every performance figure carries its anchor inside the sentence: the RFP threshold ("EMR is 0.45, well below the RFP requirement of 1.0"), the industry range ("multiplier of 2.6, compared to a typical industry range of 3.0–3.2"), the client's own scale, or the prior state ("compared to the previous operating approach"). Scope quantities (MGD, miles, hours offered) are exempt. The pilot anchored once ("$1.6 billion — 32 times the $50 million threshold") and left TRIR, EMR, D&B 5A3, and the portfolio counts floating.
**Test:** for each performance figure (rate, record, savings, ranking, revenue against a threshold), point to the comparator in the same sentence, and fail any figure without one.

**13. Bold the consequence, not the feature; close paragraphs on the client's outcome.** At least 35% of body paragraphs end on a two- or three-outcome clause built on ensure / deliver / reduce / improve / protect / strengthen ("…ensuring consistent, responsive, and reliable service"), usually bolded; no paragraph ends on a bracket, hedge, or caveat. Bold density is 10–30 runs per 1,000 words (Hull 32, Santa Monica 11; pilot 5.6, all labels), and at least 70% of bold runs contain a number, a client noun, or an outcome verb. Benefit attaches as a participial or purpose tail or as one labeled sidebar per subsection, never as a "Why this matters" sub-head.
**Test:** read only the bold text on a page (it must read as a chain of client outcomes), classify each bold run as outcome/proof or label (≥ 70% outcome), and count paragraphs ending on a benefit clause (≥ 35%).

**14. Requirement → our figure → exceeds, in prose.** Each RFP desired qualification is answered in a sentence carrying the requirement, the Jacobs figure, and an exceedance verb: "We currently operate 106 wastewater facilities across 69 clients, including 50 facilities with capacities exceeding 3 MGD, demonstrating experience well beyond the requirement to operate more than 10 facilities of this size." A compliance table repeats the answer; it never replaces it. The pilot answered only in Table 3-7, two rows of which were placeholders.
**Test:** for each RFP qualification, find one prose sentence with all three parts, and fail any qualification answered only in a table.

**15. Four registers of proof.** Under every major heading, proof appears as (1) an inline number in prose, (2) a stat callout or exhibit pointed to in bold ("shown in Exhibit 2-4"), (3) a table row, and (4) a named, titled third-party voice; at least three of the four appear within any two consecutive pages. Hull's executive summary pages 7–8 carry all four (Westerly $134k inline, the $5 million value wheel, Exhibit 2-1 rows, the Fath-Azam quote). The pilot used registers 1 and 3 only: ten tables, no exhibit pointers, no callout statistic, no named quote.
**Test:** tick the four registers for each pair of consecutive pages and fail any pair with fewer than three.

**16. Numbers per sentence; lists longer than four become exhibits.** At most three numeric facts per sentence; a facility-scoping sentence ("a 3.07-MGD wastewater treatment facility, seven pump stations, a stormwater pumping station, 42 miles of gravity sewer, a low-pressure system, and an ocean outfall") may carry five. Four or more facts otherwise, or any inline list of more than four items, becomes bullets, a table row, or a map callout ("398 Staff in Massachusetts · 712 Staff in New England"). Overall density 4.5–6.5 numbers per 100 words (winners 5.66 and 5.47). Pilot: an eleven-system mileage string and an eleven-site tenure list inline.
**Test:** count numeric tokens per sentence (fail over 5, or over 3 outside a scoping sentence) and items per inline list (fail over 4).

**17. Reference narratives, not key-value tables.** A reference block gives size / years / fee / contact, three or more italic relevance tags ("EPA Region 1 · Odor Control Study · Large Collection System"), then Services provided / Facilities managed / Accomplishments, and every Accomplishments block carries a dollar or percent ("$500,000 in savings", "reduced electrical consumption by more than 30%"). Narrative is first-person past-tense actions → quantified result → one relevance clause ("comparable in size and complexity to the Town of Hull's system"); at most one sentence names the target client, and none analogizes its procurement, settlement, or politics.
**Test:** each reference shows ≥ 3 relevance tags, ≥ 1 quantified accomplishment, and ≤ 1 sentence naming the target client.

**18. Let the client say the number.** Prefer client-attributed outcomes ("The City estimated cost savings of up to $12.7 million compared to the previous operating approach") and named, titled quotes ("— Firooz Fath-Azam, Former South Huron Valley System Manager") to self-claims. An unnamed paraphrase ("city leaders have said publicly…") is cut, not kept.
**Test:** every testimonial or attributed figure carries name, title, and organization, and unnamed paraphrases count 0.

**19. Dollarize the give.** Every value-add carries a dollar value or a measurable quantity ("2,000 hours of dedicated support annually", "1,000 hours/year of discounted engineering", a 2.6 multiplier, a 20-minute response), the items sum to one headline ("$5 million in value at no additional cost over the 10-year term"; "$4.1M included in our base fee"), and each is tied to a client priority (SmartCover → I/I; jetter → blockages). A callout that commits only to a reporting cadence carries no value.
**Test:** every value-add or callout commitment carries a number or dollar value and names the client priority it serves.

### Client address

**20. The client opens every section as grammatical subject; Jacobs is absent from sentence one.** "The Town of Hull's residents, businesses, and coastal environment depend on wastewater operations that protect public health…"; "The City of Santa Monica is setting the national standard for sustainable, locally sourced water…". Jacobs first appears in sentence two or later. "The City gets…" puts the client in the subject slot but makes Jacobs the topic, and fails.
**Test:** the subject of the first sentence under each H2 is the client or its assets, and its verb is not gets, is buying, or contracts with.

**21. The client is beneficiary, never buyer; the relationship is partnership.** The client sits in the benefit slot of Jacobs sentences: "providing the Town with…", "the Town will gain…", "so the City will have real-time visibility", "representing a significant cost savings to the Town". Banned as client-facing verbs and nouns in narrative: gets, is buying, contracts with, counterparty, contract party, balance sheet, threshold (the legal-entity and financial tables excepted). Relationship nouns are partner, extension of City staff, one team, shoulder-to-shoulder. Pilot: "The City gets…" four times, "The City is buying one thing", "one California corporation and one balance sheet".
**Test:** grep the buyer list in narrative prose (0 hits) and confirm the client appears as object of gain / receive / have / provide at least once per subsection.

**22. Jacobs is "Jacobs", "we/our", "our team", or "the on-site team"; second person is spent on respect.** Never "the operator", "the contractor", "the proposer", or "they" for Jacobs in own-voice prose ("the Offeror" is permitted once in legal boilerplate). "You/your" appears only where the sentence names something the client owns, values, or will receive ("your visionary investment", "your single point of contact", "names your staff already know"), never in a sentence naming a shortcoming, and never dropped into an otherwise third-person paragraph ("the operator will tell you first when they do not" fails twice).
**Test:** grep "the operator", "the contractor", "the proposer", and "they" referring to Jacobs (0), and check that each "you/your" sentence names a client-owned object with no shortcoming and no third-person client reference in the same sentence.

**23. Needs are aspirations or operating conditions, never lacks; site deficits go to the approach section.** "The Town is seeking more than regulatory compliance. It is looking for stronger leadership, clearer communication, and a proactive asset-management approach"; "These assets operate under conditions shaped by tidal influence, wet-weather variability, and seasonal demand, requiring proactive management and operational discipline." Deficiencies observed on a site visit appear only in the technical approach as Observed issue → Improvement → Expected outcome, paired with credit for what the plant has sustained ("Despite the data challenges, the facility has maintained NPDES compliance"). Pilot: "gives the City what it has lacked", "the City's over-stretched program", a five-item deficiency list in the qualifications opener.
**Test:** in Qualifications, count lacks / lacked / over-stretched / failing / deferred / inconsistent applied to the client (0), and in the approach confirm every observed deficiency has an improvement and an expected outcome beside it.

**24. Commitment grammar.** [Named person or role] + will + operational verb + object + cadence or deadline (+ artifact): "Project Manager Nathan Callison will maintain open day-to-day coordination with Town staff"; "Mack Mckenzie, our CPO, will serve as your single point of contact"; "increase Ocean Avenue vac-truck cleaning to every eight weeks"; "answered by a person who will respond within 20 minutes". Cadence words: Day 1, daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, annually, within 72 hours, January 2026. Present tense is for standing facts ("Jacobs operates 106 wastewater facilities"). Commitment verbs are observable (deploy, deliver, hold, issue, route, invite, validate, cross-train, retain); benefit verbs are strengthens, reduces, improves, protects, positions; rhetorical verbs (proves, answers, sets apart, discloses) are not commitments. Pilot: "each quarter the Jacobs project manager gives…", "the Jacobs project manager delivers a monthly compliance report…".
**Test:** tag each commitment sentence with owner, "will", operational verb, and cadence, and fail any present-tense or ownerless commitment.

**25. Unshippable content; intensifiers earn their number.** Every subsection carries at least two of: (a) a named person with credential and current role ("Mack Mckenzie (WW Grade V, AWT3 progressing to AWT5)"); (b) a named Jacobs tool or program (SmartCover®, WATS modeling, Six-Point Compliance Program, BeyondZero, Annual Innovation Workshop, Hach WIMS); (c) a client-specific identifier in the client's own vocabulary (Baykeeper Settlement Agreement, the 2025 Cease and Desist Order, NexGen, Keller Beach Pump Station); (d) a quantified included offer ("2,000 hours of dedicated support annually"). Any comparative or superlative (significant, industry leader, extensive) sits in the same sentence as the number or named comparison that earns it ("TRIR of 0.89, which is significantly below industry averages").
**Test:** ask of each paragraph "could a competitor paste this unchanged?" (no), count items (a)–(d) per subsection (≥ 2), and fail any intensifier without a number or named comparison in its sentence.

### Win-theme threading

**26. Name the themes once, then repeat them as headings.** Three to five win themes are named once (cover letter or an executive-summary exhibit such as Hull's Exhibit 2-1), then each recurs verbatim as a section heading, a callout title, and a closer, and is re-mapped as a row in the approach goals table. Each theme phrase appears at least three times across cover letter, executive summary, and section headings. The pilot stated three themes in its italic deck (California operator, auditable compliance, incumbent replacement) and never turned one into a heading.
**Test:** count each theme phrase across the document (≥ 3 occurrences each, at least one as a heading).

**27. Signature phrases repeat; sentence frames do not.** Three to five signature phrases ("extension of City staff", "more than an operator", "no surprises", "defensible compliance", "Day 1") each appear at least twice. Any other 3+-word frame appears at most once per section ("The City gets…" ×4, "one thing" ×2, "line by line" ×2 are tics, not themes).
**Test:** run a 3-gram frequency scan and require ≥ 3 signature phrases at ≥ 2 occurrences with no non-signature 3-gram repeated within one section.

### Handling the incumbent and concessions

**28. Never name a competitor or incumbent.** Say "the previous contract operator", "the previous provider", "the previous operating approach", "traditional O&M providers", "something no typical O&M firm offers", "qualified incumbent employees". Competition is handled by a Traditional-vs-Jacobs comparison table (Hull Exhibit 2-3), never by name and never by a swipe at ownership models ("rather than the short-term return that private-equity models typically prioritize"). The pilot named the incumbent six times.
**Test:** competitor and incumbent firm names count 0 and ownership-model swipes count 0.

**29. Frame the incumbent's failures as the client's aspirations.** "But the Town is seeking more than regulatory compliance. It is looking for stronger leadership, clearer communication, and a proactive asset-management approach that anticipates issues before they become problems." The evaluator learns what went wrong without an accusation; no sentence attributes a deficiency to the City, its staff, or its program, and the transition is written people-first ("We will invite qualified incumbent employees to join our team to preserve hard-won site knowledge").
**Test:** read every sentence whose subject is the client, its staff, or its program, and fail any that names a shortcoming.

**30. Four-move concession; disclose only what the RFP asks.** Every required negative follows, in one paragraph: (a) scale context ("Jacobs may, from time to time, have minor environmental violations, as customarily occurs in the ordinary course of business"), (b) bounding ("minor in nature", "administrative in nature"), (c) resolution ("the applicators renewed their expired licenses… resolved in coordination with regulators"), (d) no-impact close ("with no impact to our ability to deliver services"). Disclosures use the RFP's format with a Resolution column beside every item. Nothing negative is volunteered beyond the RFP ask: no self-disclosed incident tables (pilot Table 3-10), no "We make no claim yet about long-run results", no "It is here because", no disclosure used as a device for candor.
**Test:** each disclosed negative shows all four moves in its paragraph, every negative table traces to an RFP requirement, and self-disclaimer sentences count 0.

**31. Acknowledge a weakness in a subordinate clause; answer it in the main clause.** "While not all of the reference projects are located within EPA Region 1 or New York State, they demonstrate our extensive experience operating similar facilities under comparable regulatory frameworks and performance requirements." One sentence, the evaluator's own criterion in the main clause, no dwelling ("Clovis is smaller than Richmond. It is here because…" fails).
**Test:** fail any concession longer than one sentence or lacking an answering main clause.

### Devices — what each carries and when to use it

**32. Choose the device by what it carries.** Devices repeat proof the prose has already stated; they never introduce a claim the prose lacks.

| Device | Use when | Shape | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stat callout | One figure carries a theme and has a comparator | Number + comparator + consequence in 8–20 words, and the figure also appears in prose ("Discounted engineering multiplier of 2.6 against an industry range of 3.0–3.2, a saving to the Town over the contract life") | ≤ 1 per page |
| Fact box / benefit sidebar ("Benefits to Richmond") | Closing a subsection | Verbless triad of outcomes, 15–30 words, zero feature or tool names, client named ("Longer asset life, fewer reactive repairs, clearer budget forecasts, and faster parts availability to shorten outages") | 1 per subsection |
| Pull quote | A client has said it better than we can and we hold name + title | ≤ 35 words, name, title, organization, placed beside the theme it proves ("— Firooz Fath-Azam, Former South Huron Valley System Manager") | ≤ 1 per major section; never unnamed |
| Commitment callout | A promise with owner, cadence, artifact, and value | "[Person or role] will [verb] [object] [cadence]" plus a number or dollar value | ≤ 2 per section |
| Table | An RFP-mandated form, or ≥ 4 items with ≥ 3 attributes each | Numbered caption ("Table 3-1."); rightmost column is the client's outcome ("Value to the City"); prose states the claim and points to the table | Table share ≤ 0.35 of words, aim ≤ 0.25 |
| Map / footprint exhibit | Staff counts, tenures, sites | Callouts on the map ("398 Staff in Massachusetts · 712 Staff in New England"); replaces any inline list of more than four items | 1 per section |
| Org chart | Chain of authority | Client at top, names and titles, reach-back groups as dotted lines, pointed to in prose | 1 per proposal |
| Bulleted list | 3–8 parallel items | ≤ 6-word bold lead-in + en dash or colon + 15–40 words; all "We will…" or all noun phrases; numbered only for sequenced steps | — |
| Traditional-vs-Jacobs table | Competition without names | "Traditional O&M provider" column vs "Jacobs" column; rows are the client's priorities | 1 per proposal |
| Value wheel / dollarized stack | Value-adds included in the fee | Each item with a dollar value or quantity, summed to one headline ("$5 million in value at no additional cost") | 1 per proposal, in the executive summary |

**Test:** each device on a page matches its row for use, shape, and limit, and no device carries a claim absent from the prose.

**33. Exhibits support the prose; the rightmost column is the client's outcome.** The claim lives in a sentence; the exhibit is pointed to in bold ("shown in Exhibit 2-4", "as summarized in Exhibit 5-1"); every figure and table carries a numbered caption in brand format ("Table 3-1."); the rightmost column of every multi-column proof exhibit names the client's outcome ("Value to the Town", "Benefit to the Town", "Expected Outcome", "Strategies to Deliver Results"). About every third paragraph points to an exhibit or numbered section. Pilot: Table 3-8 ends in "Processes", the reference tables have no outcome column, and no exhibit is pointed to in bold.
**Test:** each exhibit has a pointer sentence in prose, a numbered caption, and an outcome-named rightmost column.

### Banned words and patterns

**34. Banned vocabulary and patterns.** Count must be 0 in body prose.

- **Hype (the `metrics.py` list):** world-class, best-in-class, leverage (as a verb), synergy, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, robust, seamless, holistic, utilize, passionate, unparalleled, proven track record, we are pleased, as a leading, comprehensive suite.
- **Buyer framing of the client:** gets, is buying, contracts with, counterparty, contract party, balance sheet, threshold (as what the client wants).
- **Deficit language about the client:** lacks, lacked, what it has lacked, over-stretched, failing, deferred, inconsistent, what the City has been missing.
- **Forensic frame:** proof, prove(s), evidence that, that test, answers the question, the case, scrutiny, the jury.
- **Meta-narration:** this section, what follows, set(s) … apart, the question … is really, organized around, is here because, two facts.
- **Self-disclaimers:** we make no claim, we cannot yet, it is too early, contacts and fees are as last confirmed.
- **Aphorisms:** any sentence whose grammatical subject is an indefinite generic noun (a firm, a contract, nothing, one) and which contains no proper noun, digit, or named action ("A strained contract is usually a starved one.").
- **Bolted-on so-what:** any sub-head or run-in beginning "Why this matters".
- **Competitor and incumbent names**, and ownership-model swipes.
- **Intensifiers without a number in the same sentence:** significant(ly), industry leader, extensive, unmatched, deep.
- **Repeated frames:** "The City gets…", "one thing", "line by line", or any non-signature 3-gram used twice in a section.
- **Brand mechanics:** serial comma omitted; "#" for number (use №); "Jacobs Solutions Inc." outside legal text; JS or JE; emoji; unspaced em dash; hyphen for a range (use en dash); straight quotes; double space after a period.

**Test:** grep each list against body prose (0 hits), and for aphorisms and intensifiers read the flagged sentence for a proper noun, digit, or named comparison.

### What never appears in body text

**35. Zero editorial residue.** Body prose carries no square brackets of any kind ([PLACEHOLDER], [VERIFY], [CONFIRM], [TBD], [FIGURE …], [CALLOUT …]), no draft notes, source lists, or decision lists, and no hedge attached to a figure ("99.8% [VERIFY — 99.98% in other sources]"). Conflicting figures are reconciled in the fact pack, not in the sentence; until reconciled, the prose states the figure at the precision both sources support ("more than 300 miles", "at least 98%", "approximately $12 billion") and the conflict is logged in a draft-notes footer below a horizontal rule that is stripped before layout. An unconfirmed person or role is written as confirmed past fact or cut, never tagged. Hull carried $12B and $16B revenue on different pages and never showed the seam; winners have 0 tags, the pilot 52 tags and 14.9 brackets per 1,000 words.
**Test:** grep "[" in body prose (0) and "Draft notes", "Sources used", "Decisions needed", "VERIFY", "PLACEHOLDER" above the footer rule (0), and fail any figure followed by a bracket or hedging parenthesis.
