# Site-Visit Diagnosis of Process Control Fundamentals Across Four WWTPs

## Jacobs Understands the Process Control Challenges and Goals for [CLIENT]'s Four Facilities: Process Stability Starts with the Fundamentals

[CLIENT] — a Southcentral US municipal water utility trust operating four wastewater treatment facilities — shares a common challenge across all four plants: process control fundamentals have eroded, and the resulting instability shows up in permit excursions, reactive operations, and equipment running well outside its intended operating envelope. Our observations during site visits confirmed what the compliance record shows: these facilities aren't limited by their design. They're operationally constrained by inconsistent solids management, underutilized instrumentation, and aeration systems running without meaningful feedback or control.

- **At North Canadian**, the plant had recently experienced an upset requiring re-seeding of the biological process. Air distribution across the aeration basins was poor — low dissolved oxygen (DO) at the front of the train and excessive DO at the tail end — suggesting the facility may not always need all four basins in service. Blowers have no variable frequency drives (VFDs) and rely on manual valves for air distribution, with DO probes positioned only at the unaerated influent and end of the aeration basins. Return activated sludge/waste activated sludge (RAS/WAS) control appeared to follow a set-and-forget approach rather than active process management. The Phase 3 plant, effectively just a flow equalization basin (FEB), discharges directly to the combined effluent — a compliance risk any time untreated or septic flow reaches it. The Phase II chlorine contact basin had floating sludge with a permanently placed sludge judge, a sign of chronic solids carryover. These conditions, combined with the sulfur dioxide system's inability to match chlorine demand during high flows, have driven the facility's history of fecal coliform and chlorine residual violations.

- **At Deer Creek**, mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) appeared very light with excessive DO and early-stage foaming — indicators of a young sludge age and over-aeration. RAS pumps had VFDs but were operating in static mode, not flow-paced. Secondary clarifiers showed excessive algae growth with no covered weirs, creating conditions where sloughing algae can blind the sand filters downstream. Sand filters were being manually backwashed rather than operated on automated control. Organic loading is approaching the facility's design capacity, blower reliability has been a persistent problem (typically only two of four can run simultaneously without faulting), and the anoxic selector zones have been out of service since 2019. The sludge holding tanks that receive both WAS and primary sludge have no mixing or aeration, creating the septic conditions that drive the extreme H₂S levels in the belt press room — levels high enough that our team could not enter the room during the site visit.

- **At Chisholm Creek**, aeration basins were over-aerated with excessive DO and all blowers running — energy spent without process benefit. Both bar screens at the headworks pump station were out of service. The backwash return tank has an overflow piped directly to the effluent, a compliance risk if sampled during overflow conditions. That said, Chisholm Creek has the fewest historical permit excursions and the most resilient overall performance among the four plants.

- **South Canadian** will enter the contract term as a newly constructed 8.66-MGD activated sludge facility with on-site hypochlorite generation, disc thickeners, and new cloth filtration. The operational challenge here isn't recovery — it's getting the fundamentals right from day one, so the new plant establishes strong compliance margins and operating discipline before issues develop.

**The common thread across all four facilities is clear: biological process control, solids inventory management, aeration optimization, and instrumentation reliability are the levers that will move the needle on compliance and operational stability.** That's where our process control strategy starts. **Exhibit 1-11 briefly summarizes process control priorities** we've identified based on site visits, facility data review, and compliance record analysis. Following the exhibit, we provide a detailed discussion of our full approach by facility.
