# UV Disinfection, Solids Dewatering, and Potable-Water Protection

The plants have permit limits for bacterial indicators (fecal coliform and E. coli). These bacteria are larger than the pore size of the MBR membranes and are completely blocked if the membranes are intact. Usually, effluent limits for bacterial indicators can be met by an MBR process without downstream disinfection. However, many virus pass through the membrane pores, and this creates a human health risk not addressed by effluent limits for bacterial indicators. It is good practice to operate the downstream disinfection process to inactivate virus and to kill any bacteria present due to membrane damage. JC Solutions will always operate the UV disinfection process at its design dose to reduce the risk of pathogens on human health.

[FACILITY B] and [FACILITY A] are similar in that they produce primary sludge (from primary clarifiers) and secondary sludge (from the MBR process). Both plants lack a thickening process other than what can be achieved in the primary clarifiers or by the membranes. Primary and secondary sludges are directed to aerated holding tanks/digesters, where the solids concentration decreases somewhat as digestion occurs.

Different dewatering technologies are used at these plants—centrifuges at [FACILITY B] and screw presses at [FACILITY A]. As a minimum, these dewatering processes must be operated so cake passes a paint filter test, which is a requirement for landfill disposal. Individual landfills may have additional requirements.

It is desirable to achieve cake with higher solids concentration, which reduces hauling and disposal costs. However, there are tradeoffs between cake concentration and the way primary and secondary treatment are operated. Operating the primary clarifiers to produce thicker primary sludge tends to increase dewatered cake concentration, but it also decreases primary clarifier effluent quality, leading to cost increases in secondary treatment. Operating the MBR process to produce thicker secondary sludge would also tend to increase dewatered cake concentration, but as discussed above, it is desirable to use surface wasting to improve mixed liquor filterability and membrane performance.

There is a delicate balance of competing factors, where operation for an advantage in part of the plant results in a disadvantage elsewhere in the plant. Our O&M staff and wastewater engineers are familiar with these trade-offs and will use process simulation and digital tools to find the balance that maximizes advantages and minimizes disadvantages. **How It Benefits [CLIENT]:** Potentially reduce costs as a result of improving cake dryness and lowering chemical consumption by intelligently managing sludge production and wasting practices.

Drawings of [FACILITY A] show a double backflow preventer to isolate the potable water supply (1W) from plant reuse water (3W). This is not appropriate for a high hazard connection such as plant effluent. There is pressurized reuse water on the downstream side of the backflow preventers, and failure would result in contamination of the potable water supply. We will help [CLIENT] determine whether a reverse pressure zone (RPZ) backflow preventer is required.
