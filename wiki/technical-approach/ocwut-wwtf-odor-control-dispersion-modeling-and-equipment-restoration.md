# Treatment Facility Odor Control — Source Sampling, Dispersion Modeling, Chemical Optimization, and Equipment Restoration

At the treatment facilities, odor control will focus on process stability, equipment reliability, and targeted engineering assessment.

We will maintain aerobic conditions in critical process areas to prevent hydrogen sulfide formation. We'll actively manage solids inventory and sludge age to avoid conditions that contribute to odor generation. We'll optimize sludge handling processes to reduce retention time and minimize exposure.

For the facilities, our engineering approach relies on source sampling and air dispersion modeling. At contract start, our odor control experts will collect samples from odorous treatment processes and analyze them for hydrogen sulfide and reduced sulfur compounds. Combined with site-specific atmospheric data, dispersion modeling will identify the most likely sources of off-site odor impact at each facility, prioritize improvements for the greatest community benefit, and establish a rapid response framework when complaints occur. **We applied this same methodology in Waterbury, CT, where it directly informed a control strategy that reduced odor complaints by more than 55% in the first 2 years (Exhibit 1-21).**

When Jacobs took over operations at the City of Waterbury Water Pollution Control Facility, existing odor control systems were in disrepair, and the community was experiencing significant complaints. We conducted an odor study, identified major sources, and implemented a short-term control plan that decreased odor complaints by more than 55% between Year 1 and Year 2. Long-term improvements included a carbon absorber polishing stage, new chemical dosing pumps, replacement of aging air piping, and a $2M odor control systems upgrade completed in partnership with Walsh Engineers.

On the chemical side, we'll build on what's already in place. Ferric chloride is currently used across the facilities for hydrogen sulfide control, but site observations indicate its application is inconsistent and not optimized. We'll evaluate the performance of existing chemical programs at each facility, including ferric chloride dosing strategies, and identify opportunities to improve effectiveness or reduce cost through alternative approaches. We'll evaluate any changes against life-cycle cost and operational impact before implementation.

The current state of odor control equipment is a safety concern as much as an odor concern. At North Canadian, 30 odor scrubbers are installed, but only three are operational. At Deer Creek, seven are installed with only three operational. Site visit observations confirmed that H₂S levels in multiple buildings were so high that areas could not be entered, and some gas monitoring systems were unprogrammed or non-functional. Operators are working in conditions that exceed safe exposure thresholds because the systems designed to protect them aren't running. **Jacobs will prioritize restoration of odor control and ventilation equipment at both facilities as a safety and odor control obligation, coordinated with [CLIENT]'s CIP program to address the systemic equipment failures identified in the odor control evaluation.** Headworks and solids handling areas carry the highest combined safety and odor risk, so we'll address these first.

*Exhibit 1-21. We implemented a successful odor control mitigation strategy with our client in Waterbury, CT. Using the same methodology we plan to apply in [CLIENT], we identified and prioritized nine key odor sources and cost-effectively minimized them.*
