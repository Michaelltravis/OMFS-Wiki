# Collection System Odor Control — WATS Modeling, Wet-Well Optimization, and Wet-Weather Basin Management

Odor control begins upstream. At [CLIENT] — a multi-plant regional wastewater utility with extensive force main conveyance — our site observations confirmed that a significant portion of odor originates in the collection system before flow ever reaches the treatment facilities. Long force mains and pump stations, including major conveyance from the Witcher Pump Station, create extended detention times that allow septicity and hydrogen sulfide formation to develop well upstream of the plants. Controlling odor at its source is more effective and more efficient than managing it at the fence line (**Exhibit 1-19**).

Day-to-day operations structured around our "**prevent, detect, respond, and improve" framework** will address these upstream drivers through optimized wet-well management, refined chemical dosing, and integrated field data. But operational control alone isn't enough to solve a system with [CLIENT]'s complexity and history. We will bring a second, deeper capability: the engineering expertise to quantify odor and corrosion across the system, determine off-site impacts, evaluate control alternatives, and select the most cost-effective solutions before committing resources. This is what separates a managed response from a solved problem. **Leading this engineering effort is Adrian Romero-Flores, PhD, our Odor Control SME. Adrian brings 10 years of expertise spanning sewer process modeling, collection system odor control, wastewater treatment processes, and biosolids management and has been directly engaged in shaping our approach for [CLIENT] from the earliest stages of proposal development.**

This engineering approach applies across both the collection system and the four treatment facilities with the tools matched to each context. For the collection system, the foundation is sewer process modeling using WATS, which is the only model in the world proven on a significant number of projects to accurately predict odors, corrosion, and the performance of mitigation alternatives across complex collection systems. **We've applied WATS on more than 35 projects, collaborating with Aalborg University and participating in the $20M Australian Sewer Corrosion and Odor Research (SCORe) program to help refine and validate the model.** WATS simulates sulfide generation, odor release, and corrosion risk across the entire network, identifying hotspots not visible through field observation alone (**Exhibit 1-20**). It can also be linked with wastewater treatment process models to determine how upstream conditions affect plant performance, directly supporting our integrated approach at [CLIENT]. We will use WATS to evaluate chemical dosing strategies, optimize dosing locations and rates, and assess alternatives before implementation, eliminating the costly trial-and-error currently observed in operations and delivering meaningful cost savings on chemical spend.

We will focus on upstream operational drivers in parallel. We'll optimize wet-well management by adjusting cleaning frequency, improving pump cycling, and reducing detention time, limiting septicity at the source. We'll integrate field data, alarms, and maintenance history to identify recurring problem areas and eliminate them systematically rather than responding to them repeatedly. By reducing hydrogen sulfide loading into the plants, we'll improve both odor conditions and process stability across the system, reducing the need for reactive chemical dosing and minimizing downstream impacts on solids handling and equipment.

Wet-weather events create a distinct and predictable odor risk that requires proactive management. When flows exceed treatment capacity and the wet-weather management basins at North Canadian and Witcher Pump Station are activated, settled sludge accumulates on basin floors as flow slows. As that material becomes septic, it drives significant hydrogen sulfide generation when basins are returned to service. We'll manage this risk through proactive basin management before, during, and after wet-weather events. Post-event, we'll promptly initiate drawdown and cleaning operations, consistent with Schedule 3 requirements to drain and clean wet-weather management basins no later than 7 days after the need for wet-weather storage expires. Delays beyond 21 days trigger fees at risk under the contract. We will not let it get there.
