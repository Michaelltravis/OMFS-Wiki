# Current and Future MBR Challenges with Coordinated Commissioning Need

[CLIENT] places high priority on permit compliance with substantial safety margins, particularly for phosphorus removal, even when this approach requires additional chemical usage and expense. There are plans to expand [FACILITY B] from 15 to 20 MGD, with a design-build team currently evaluating options. For [FACILITY A], future plans to expand from 32 to 38 MGD capacity would require adding a primary clarifier, a bioreactor, and more membranes. [CLIENT] is also considering a biosolids disposal project at [FACILITY A], potentially involving drying or other processing technologies.

Throughout the wastewater O&M industry, there are significant challenges in hiring and retaining qualified and certified staff, and the [CLIENT] facilities are not immune to these issues. Additionally, training and motivating a high-performing workforce requires a culture that values these important components of a comprehensive O&M delivery approach. [FACILITY B] has experienced odor complaints from nearby residents. The plant was designed with special attention to aesthetics and odor control due to proximity to expensive homes. All plants have odor-control systems, with [FACILITY B] and [FACILITY A] utilizing chemical scrubbers and GAC, while [FACILITY C] uses GAC alone for its headworks. Operations must meet odor-control requirements.

[FACILITY C]'s biosolids are hauled to [FACILITY A] for processing. A key challenge at [FACILITY A] is the lack of dedicated thickening processes for primary sludge and waste activated sludge, which limits dewatering efficiency and increases disposal costs. Future plans may include a drying facility at [FACILITY A], where there is available real estate from demolished sand filters. The facilities face several optimization challenges including membrane performance management. [FACILITY A] has new Kubota flat plates, [FACILITY C]'s hollow-fiber membranes are six years old, and [FACILITY B]'s are 16 years old, which is well past expected life, requiring imminent replacement. All three plants use enhanced biological phosphorus removal and present opportunities for improvement in operations. Chemical optimization opportunities also exist across the facilities, particularly in correctly dosing sodium hypochlorite and sodium hydroxide for the chemical scrubbers at [FACILITY A] and [FACILITY B] (saving approximately $500/day), proper Phos-Zorb application, and addressing the significant under-provision of sodium hydroxide for alkalinity at [FACILITY C].

Primary-clarifier maintenance at [FACILITY B] is complicated by fiberglass flights and chains that are prone to breakage when sludge blankets are deep enough for proper VFA production. Equipment-access issues exist at [FACILITY B], particularly for the influent pumps, which lack proper lifting equipment. The swing zones in bioreactors at [FACILITY A] lack nitrate probes for optimal control. The phosphorus-removal strategy balances between enhanced biological phosphorus removal (EBPR) and chemical precipitation, with [CLIENT] currently favoring chemical approaches with substantial safety margins. Chemical costs are significant, with potential opportunities for alternative dosing strategies or chemical substitutions (e.g., alum and caustic instead of Phos Sorb). Pump-station monitoring and maintenance are critical for ensuring compliance and sustaining continuous flow.

We recognize that [FACILITY A] is currently undergoing a construction upgrade, with ongoing work likely to continue after the awarding of a new operations contract. During the commissioning phase of the construction project, transition to a new O&M provider will require a seamless process to manage the operational aspects of the facility alongside the commissioning team with a goal to continue reliable, compliant and safe operations for the facilities, the onsite personnel and the community.
