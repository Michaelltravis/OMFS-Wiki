Using Jacobs’ maintenance planning best practices written as an algorithm within the Intelligent O&M suite of tools, the maintenance manager can set the schedule, revise the suggested labor pool, and revise specific work orders, and then issue the plan to staff. There are numerous filters and analysis tools that can be used to optimize metrics along with way, which were co-developed with Palantir based on ISO55000 principles and Jacobs’ expertise. The tool also provides us with sophisticated insights into where more preventive maintenance is needed based on work order history and risk, or where less is appropriate—an analysis that used to require a lot of time and the insights of a very select group of industry experts. For consulting clients, this is typically worth over $100,000 per year for setup, annual licensing, and support fees, but for this program we know it will more than pay for itself, so it is included at no additional cost to the County.

The latest module added to the Intelligent O&M suite of tools uses large language model (LLM) AI on the Palantir Foundry platform to ingest and read only a specific plant’s documentation and give users a search engine style interface to ask plain English questions and get answers in seconds. First deployed at our project at the Spokane County, WA, Regional WWTP, the system is designed to only use site-specific information, but in any format—CMMS, electronic manuals, PDF files, Word documents, scanned documents, CAD drawings, etc. The AI Assistant ( **Exhibit 2-8** ) only uses this library for answering questions, and nothing is obtained from the internet unless it is specifically related to what the maintenance team wants to know.

Wastewater treatment facilities have thousands of assets, and nearly every one of them comes with an equipment manual that defines what type of lubricant, repair parts, preventive maintenance, and other details are critical to maximizing the life of the asset. Much of this essential, frequently needed information like maintenance schedules, will be built into the CMMS. The maintenance team will still need to regularly refer to the vendor manuals, each being possibly hundreds of pages in length. Combined with the total number of assets, a mechanic might be faced with finding a needle in a haystack of information on a random shelf where plant documents are kept. The same kind of sorting problem happens when looking at equipment repair histories in CMMS —tools to support mechanics with this kind of plant-specific information have been unavailable. **Until now.**
